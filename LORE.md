# LORE.md

The world of `/pp`. Everything that tells a story (class lore cards, shop
items, quests, places, future activities) draws from this page, and anything
new it invents is written back here first. Facts here outrank flavour text
elsewhere: if a card contradicts this page, the card is wrong.

## The feel

A great city at the turn of the 1700s: guilds and counting houses, coffee
rooms and broadsheets, debtors' courts, chartered companies, gin lanes and
gilt salons a street apart. It is crowded, unequal and loud. Magic is not
rare or holy. It is a trade, a nuisance and a fact of life, like weather or
interest, and nobody in the city finds it strange that the market's great
cauldron mints money.

Think of Sigil in Planescape: a city that is a world of its own, run by
factions with philosophies, where every street has someone working an angle.
The people are birds: every kind of bird, in every trade and every gutter.

### How it reads

- **Lore, not poetry.** Give names, places, prices, institutions and
  grudges. A sentence should teach a fact about the world. A mood comes from
  facts, never from adjectives.
- **The weird is mundane.** A bell that rings under the sea at the drowning
  hour is a nuisance to the fishmongers, not a wonder.
- **Money and height.** Who owes whom, and who lives above whom, explains
  most of what happens in the city.
- Lowercase, the bird voice, no oxford commas, and none of the punctuation
  `scripts/prose.sh` refuses.
- Real birds only. Never the fleet's own (cygnet or swan, merlin,
  peregrine, skua), and no names from tabletop games' rulebooks.

## The rookery

**The rookery** is the city, and the name is meant both ways: a roost of
birds and a slum. It is wrapped around the foot and lower flanks of **the
spire**, a tower of grey stone far older than anyone who lives on it.

Nobody built the spire. The flock found it standing. A road of steps called
**the stair** winds up its outside, and the city clings to it: timber
galleries bolted to the stone, brick guildhalls on corbels, houses built on
the roofs of other houses, rope bridges, hoists and laddered alleys. New
rooms are tacked on wherever a beam will hold, and every few years a
gallery falls. Close to the stone the brick is old and fine. Further out it
turns to planks, canvas and rope.

**Height is wealth.** A ledge on the stair is called a **perch**, and perches
are owned, rented, mortgaged and fought over. The higher the perch, the older
the money. Climbing the stair means climbing in the world, and the city
says it both ways.

### The quarters, from the top down

- **The great nothingness.** Where the stair runs out, at the top of the
  spire, there is nothing: no wind, no sound and no sky, only a stillness
  that holds weight. Those who have stood in it don't talk about it. The
  topmost perch is gilded where the nothing touches it.
- **The gilt.** The highest perches. Old families, the order of the stair's
  chapter house and the counting house's private rooms. Quiet, clean, dry
  and guarded.
- **The bellward.** The middle stair. Guildhalls, the playhouses, coffee
  rooms, the broadsheet presses, the kiln keepers' showrooms and the
  respectable trades. Most of what happens in the city is decided here.
- **The pot.** The great market square at the spire's foot, and the cauldron
  in its middle. See below.
- **The sump.** The lowest dry streets on the seaward side: tanneries,
  rendering yards, gin cellars, rooming houses ten to a room, fighting pits,
  fences and the taverns where pp are wagered. The city's crime lives here,
  and so do most of its people.
- **The low town.** Below the sump, under the sea. It went under in one
  night, the **drowning**, and the tide still covers and uncovers its roofs.
  The old chapel bell rang as the water came in and has rung at that hour
  ever since, from wherever its pieces are.
- **The hedge.** A wall of thorn planted around the landward side of the
  city against the mire, kept by the hedge company.

### The pot

**The melting pot** is a cauldron of black iron as wide as a house, sunk
into the paving of the square at the spire's foot. It was dragged up out of
the surf after the first rookery fell, by an albatross with a harness and a
season to spare, and nobody knows who made it or what it was for.

What goes into the pot does not come out the same. A day's work, a long
talk, a night's watch, all of it poured in comes back as **pot points**, the
city's scrip, which every stall and every debt court takes. The ravens of
the counting house read the steam and keep the ledger of what goes in and
what comes out. The pot always keeps a share, the **levy**, which goes to the
city's treasury, and what is spent climbing the stair goes back into the
pot and is gone. The city runs on pp, and the pot is why it runs at all.

Quests are notices pinned to the board at the pot's rim. Newcomers sleep
against its warm side, a custom that is older than the city's laws.

### The hearth

Beside the pot stands **the hearth**, a public fire tended by the herons of
the hearthkeepers. They light it only on a day the whole city has earned it,
when more has gone into the pot than on an ordinary day, and on a lit day
everyone's work goes further. Whether that's a rule of the fire or a rule of
the herons is argued over in every coffee room.

What counts as ordinary is kept on a **notch stick**. Each time the pot turns
over, before dawn, a novice cuts the day just gone into the stick and shaves
the oldest notch off, so it never holds more than seven. A good week raises
the mark and a lean week lowers it. The keepers read the steam for talk and
work poured in at the rim, and leave the notices on the board out of the
count. The fire has three heights, and once lit it isn't let down until the
next day is out, whatever the city does after.

### Chirographs and round robins

Most notices on the board are one bird's work. Some are taken in company,
and the rookery has two ways of signing for that.

A **chirograph** is a notice taken by two. The board's clerk writes it out
twice on one sheet, writes a word across the gap between the copies and cuts
the sheet through the word along a wavy line, and each bird keeps a half.
Nobody but the clerk knows the word. When the work is done they bring both
halves to the pot's rim, and a raven of the counting house fits them
together. If the word and the teeth match, the pot pays each of them a
little over what the notice is worth. The ravens enter the extra as **the
joinder** and have never agreed on why the pot pays it. The sump says the
pot likes company.

A **round robin** is a notice signed by three or four birds in a ring, so
that no name stands first. The ferrymen and the pit crews sign their
petitions that way, because the tally board can't hang a ringleader it
can't find. A round robin pays a little more again, and the ravens record
it without trying to say who led.

The work of either is the same: time spent together, where the city can
see it. What differs is how each bird signs. Every bird brings the mark of
what they came from, and the clerks at the rim have learned to read them
all:

- **The wanderer** writes a navigator's fix, the latitude and longitude of
  where they stand, the way every entry in the kittiwake's log began.
- **The fighter** presses a thumb of pale clay, the hedge company's face
  paint. A squad signs for its rations that way, so nobody can say which
  shrike ate twice.
- **The ranger** writes a number. The wardens count every tree in the last
  wood and they count their company the same way.
- **The sorcerer** can't sign with the counting house's mark any more, so
  they press the brand on their quill hand into the wax. The house can't
  refuse a mark it made itself.
- **The thief** presses a ring brought up from the low town, never the same
  ring twice. The wet guild keeps a book of whose rings they were.
- **The bard** writes a bar of music. Anyone in the choir can read who wrote
  it, and the clerks have learned to hum.
- **The monk** leaves a grey ring of hearth ash and water. They walked out
  of the hearthkeepers, and they still carry the ash.
- **The knight** writes their name and their rung in order blue, as the
  order requires on every writ, and dates it by the drowned bell.

Signing in company teaches a bird things about their own kind that nobody
says out loud. Each class learns three, one at a time: the first time they
sign a chirograph, at their tenth signature, and the first time they close a
round robin with all four places filled.

- **Wanderer.** first: the board's clerk is an albatross too, and asks the
  same question at the rim that everyone from the grey north asks. tenth:
  the north sea company buys up chirographs signed by albatrosses, because a
  bird who signs in company can be found again. a full ring: the kittiwake
  sailed with a round robin pinned under her mast. nobody at the company
  will say what it asked for.
- **Fighter.** first: the clay is from the mire's edge, and it only sets on
  a shrike's thumb. tenth: the hedge company pays a widow's portion on every
  thumb it can match to a squad roll, and the kiln keepers pay more for the
  same rolls. a full ring: the squad that held the eastern hedge in the
  rising signed a round robin the night before. four thumbs. one came back.
- **Ranger.** first: the wardens number birds from the north gate inward,
  and the rookery has passed nine thousand. tenth: the last wood has one
  more tree in the count than in the wood, and every warden knows which.
  a full ring: wardens on a cold trail sign in fours, one at each point of
  the compass, so whatever comes can't come from behind.
- **Sorcerer.** first: the brand is the house's eye over a ledger line, and
  the line is a real entry in a real book. tenth: the ravens who fit the
  halves together are the reckoners who struck them off. they say nothing.
  they pay. a full ring: the counting house was founded on a round robin,
  and the founding ledger has four signatures and five hands.
- **Thief.** first: the rings are the drowned's, and the wet guild returns
  any a family asks for, for a fee. tenth: one ring has come back from the
  board three times with a different magpie's name on it. a full ring: the
  chapel bell was brought up under a round robin. the fourth signature was
  the bell ringer's, and he went down with the low town.
- **Bard.** first: the bar is from the song the choir sang through the
  first winter, a note longer for every name. tenth: the tally board has
  fined a bard for a bar of music signed under someone else's notice. the
  bard paid in verse. a full ring: four nightingales signing together
  make a chord, and the choir won't say which.
- **Monk.** first: the ash is from the hearth, and the hearthkeepers know
  it on sight. tenth: the herons sign nothing in their order. they sign the
  board's notices because the board feeds the sump. a full ring: in the
  rising, four herons stood in the water at the four corners of the hedge
  and didn't sign anything. the notice they were answering is still on the
  board.
- **Knight.** first: every writ the order serves is dated by the bell, and
  so is every knight's vigil. tenth: a knight who signs in company has to
  enter it at the chapter house, and the chapter house reads every entry.
  a full ring: the order's founding oath was a round robin of four cranes.
  three of the names are on the chapter house wall, among those who didn't
  come down.

### Liveries

A **livery** is the colour a bird wears in the street, and in the rookery it
says what you belong to before you open your beak. Anyone may wear a trade's
or a faction's colours. It shows where your custom goes, not an oath, and
they are glad of the advertising.

Colour comes from **the dyers' yard** in the sump, where the vats boil day and
night and the run-off turns the gutters three colours at once. Dye fades in
the sea air, so the yard doesn't sell a colour, it leases it: a livery is paid
by the week, and one that isn't paid for fades in three days and is gone.

The sump's own trades wear cheap, fast colours the yard boils by the barrel:

- **The chandlers**, who render the yards' fat into candles and still pour
  the tallow perches, wear tallow yellow.
- **The tanners** wear hide red, the colour of what comes out of the pits
  behind the rendering yards.
- **The pits**, the fighting rings under the gin cellars, wear bruise.
  Nobody who fights there chose the colour.
- **The gin lanes** wear juniper, after the berries the stills burn through
  by the cartload.
- **The ferrymen**, who row the sump's flooded streets and the low town's
  roofs at low tide, wear tidewater.
- **The fish market** wears pewter, the grey of the scales on every board
  and the coins that change hands over them.

The great factions' colours are dearer, because the yard pays the factions
for the right to sell them: counting house ink, order of the stair blue,
hedge company thorn, wet guild sea green, hearthkeepers ember, choir rose,
wardens' moss and kiln keepers' pale glass.

What a bird may wear is set by **the sumptuary roll**, which the order of the
stair keeps and enforces with writs. Height decides it:

- From the tallow perch, a sump trade's colours.
- From the shale perch, any faction's colours.
- From the chitin perch, a **plain dye** of your own.
- From the pale-glass perch, **shot silk**, a weave of two colours that turns
  from one to the other as the bird moves.
- From the drowned-bell perch, **nacre**, scraped from shells the wet guild
  brings up from the low town. The dyers can't colour it: it shows every
  colour the shell held, and nobody asks whose shells they were. The same
  perch may sew on a **device**, the small badge worn at the shoulder.
- Past the bell a livery doesn't fade. The yard
  leases it by the season instead of the week, and nobody up there has ever
  said why the dye holds.

A livery of your own needs a name, and the name and any device are granted
by **the college of heralds**. A bird petitions with the fee, and the heralds
read every petition. They refuse a name that mocks a family, copies another
bird's arms or says what shouldn't be said in the bellward, and they return
the fee of anyone they refuse. A granted livery is entered in the college's
roll, and the name is the bird's to wear for as long as the lease is paid.
The roll has room for only so many names at once, and the heralds charge
more as it fills.

## Beyond the hedge

- **The mire.** A drowned country of reed, peat and black water to the south
  and east. It rose once, in the **rising**, and nearly took the city.
  Things come up out of it that have no names. The kiln keepers burn its
  peat on its edge and blow pale glass from the ash.
- **The last wood.** Old forest beyond the mire, kept by the goshawk
  wardens, who have counted every tree in it since the first winter.
- **The grey north.** The far sea to the north: ice, fog, whale roads and
  ships that don't come back. Albatrosses come in from it and go back to it.
- **The tally mines.** Deep workings in the cliff under the sump, reached
  through the lower reaches. They are a penal colony: debtors and convicts
  go down to work off what they owe, a **tally** at a time, and the tally
  board decides when a debt is paid. Debt grows faster than tallies, and
  most who go down come up owing more, if they come up.

## Factions

- **The counting house** (ravens). The bank and the city's memory. They read
  the pot's steam, keep the ledger and lend against perches. A reckoner
  struck off the roll is branded on the quill hand with the house's mark, a
  raven's eye over a ledger line.
- **The order of the stair** (cranes). Knights sworn to hold the stair, rung
  by rung. In practice they guard the gilt, collect perch rents and serve
  writs. Every knight climbs to the top once, at their vigil, alone, and
  stands in the nothing until the drowned bell rings the hour. The names of
  those who don't come down are written on the chapter house wall.
- **The hedge company** (shrikes). A chartered mercenary company that holds
  the hedge against the mire, paid by the city and by whoever pays more.
- **The wet guild** (magpies). Divers and salvagers of the low town, and the
  city's biggest fence. Half of the bellward's furniture came up from the
  sea.
- **The hearthkeepers** (herons). A monastic order on the mire's edge that
  tends the hearth and feeds the sump in hard winters.
- **The choir** (nightingales). The playhouses, the broadsheet singers and
  the songs. Every monday the choir sings the week's names, and the birds
  named wear a **laurel**.
- **The wardens of the wood** (goshawks). Rangers of the last wood, hunters
  of the mire and the city's least welcome visitors.
- **The college of heralds** (jays). Keepers of the roll of liveries and
  devices. They grant a bird a colour and a name of its own, and refuse
  what would shame the bellward. Every refusal is entered too.
- **The kiln keepers.** The glassmakers' guild of the mire's edge, rich from
  the bellward's windows, and the glass perches that let the gilt be seen.
- **The tally board.** The debt court and the keepers of the tally mines.
  Nobody joins it, and everybody owes it. An insult is a debt too: a writ
  for a verse is set at whatever the family insulted names.
- **The north sea company.** A whaling concern with offices in the
  bellward. Its ships are insured at the counting house, and a ship lost
  with all hands pays out.

## What happened

1. **Before.** The spire stands. Something with a shell climbed the stair
   before any bird did, and left its skin behind on the way up.
2. **The first rookery** is built on the sea cliff below the spire and
   slides into the sea. The survivors climb to the spire's foot and cut
   their first perches from the cliff's shale.
3. **The first winter.** No timber and no stone. The goshawks refuse to fell
   the last wood, so the flock renders fat and pours tallow perches, and
   burns its lamps low. The nightingales sing through the dark so nobody
   forgets who is still there. An albatross drags the pot up from the surf.
4. **The rising.** The mire climbs to the hedge. The hedge company holds the
   thorn and the herons stand in the water until it stops. The kiln keepers
   blow the first pale glass from the ash.
5. **The drowning.** The low town goes under in one night. The magpies spend
   three summers bringing up the chapel bell in pieces, and it is cast into
   perches.
6. **Now.** The city is at its largest, richest and most crowded, and the
   distance between the gilt and the sump has never been longer.

## How the game maps onto the world

| In the game | In the world |
| --- | --- |
| pp | pot points, the scrip the pot gives back for what's poured in |
| voice and chat | being about the city, at work and in talk |
| the levy and treasury | the pot's share, kept for the city |
| the shop and the stair | buying a perch higher up the spire; what's spent goes back into the pot |
| the hearth | the herons' public fire, lit on a day the city earns it |
| laurels | the choir's monday names |
| quests | notices on the board at the pot's rim |
| two players on one quest | a chirograph, cut in two through a word and fitted back together |
| three or four players on one quest | a round robin, signed in a ring |
| classes | who you are in the city, and what you came from |
| seasons | the city's years, counted in tides |
| colour roles | liveries, leased from the dyers' yard by the week |
| a shared colour role | a sump trade's or a faction's livery |
| your own role (name, style, colours, icon) | a livery of your own: plain dye, shot silk or nacre, with a device |
| the mods approving a role | the college of heralds granting a petition |

## Still to come

These are places, so stories can be set in them when they arrive:

- **Story exploration** into the mire, the last wood, the grey north and the
  low town.
- **Grinding** in the tally mines.
- **The tavern.** Gambling in the sump, at **the sinking house**, a tavern
  built in the hull of a ship that ran aground in the drowning.
- **Books.** Lost books turn up in the low town's wreckage, the counting
  house's sales of debtors' goods, the mines and the wild beyond the hedge.
  Reading one adds its pages to the member's lore library, beside the class
  cards, and some teach something worth having: a buff or a sum of pp. Every
  book is a piece of this page told from inside the world, by someone with a
  stake in it.
